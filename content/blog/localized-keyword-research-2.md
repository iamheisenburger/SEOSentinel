Localized keyword research means finding the search terms people use in a specific country, language, or regional variant, and checking that those terms match the intent behind the page you plan to write. A keyword that is a direct translation of your English term is often not the phrase your customers type. The practical work is to collect candidate terms from the market itself, classify what each searcher wants, and decide which pages to build for which audience.

This article explains a method you can run yourself, the checks you can do without special tools, and the trade-offs in deciding how far to localize. The steps below are an author-proposed evaluation framework, not an established industry benchmark. Test each step against your own data before relying on it.

## Why a translated keyword list is not enough

Most teams start by translating their existing keyword list. That approach has three predictable gaps:

- **Different wording for the same need.** People in different markets often describe the same product or problem with different nouns, brand names, or abbreviations. A term that is common in one country may be rare or unused in another.
- **Different intent behind the same words.** A phrase can be a comparison query in one market and a price query in another. The page you write has to match the dominant intent in that market, not the one you assumed.
- **Different spelling and regional variants.** Spelling differences (for example, British and American English) and local terms for the same thing can split search demand across variants. You need to know which variant your audience uses before choosing a headline.

The fix is not to abandon your English list. It is to treat each market as its own research problem and collect terms from inside that market.

## Step 1: Decide which markets justify separate research

Localization has a cost. Every additional language or regional variant means more pages to write, more facts to confirm, and more pages to keep current.

A simple decision test you can run yourself:

1. List the countries or language groups where you already receive traffic, inquiries, or sales. Use your own analytics, not assumptions.
2. For each one, note whether your product is actually available and supported there (shipping, legal requirements, currency, service coverage).
3. Mark only the markets where both demand and fulfilment are real. Treat the rest as later candidates.

If a market fails the second check, keyword demand alone does not justify a page. A page that attracts visitors you cannot serve creates a support problem, not a customer.

## Step 2: Collect candidate terms from inside the market

For each market you keep, build a seed list in the local language and register. Good sources that do not require paid tools include:

- **Search suggestions.** Type your seed term into the search box of the local search engine for that country and note the autocomplete suggestions. Repeat with question starters in the local language, such as the local equivalents of "how," "what," "is," and "cost."
- **Customer language.** Read reviews, support emails, sales call notes, and chat logs from customers in that market. Record the exact words they use for the problem, the product category, and the outcome they want.
- **Local competitors' page titles and headings.** Note how businesses in that market describe their services. You are looking for vocabulary, not for copying content.
- **Your own site search logs**, if your site is already available in that language.
- **Search Console query data** for any page that already gets impressions from that country, filtered by country. This shows you the phrasing that Google is already matching to your content.

Write each candidate term in the local language exactly as a searcher would type it. Do not translate it back into English during collection. Translation is useful later for your review team, but the search term itself must stay in the market's own wording.

## Step 3: Classify the intent of each term

Once you have a list, classify each term by what the searcher is trying to do. A common, simple set of categories you can use as a working framework is:

- **Informational:** the person wants to learn something ("how long does a process take").
- **Commercial investigation:** the person is comparing options ("type A versus type B").
- **Transactional:** the person is ready to buy, book, or sign up ("book appointment near me").
- **Navigational:** the person is looking for a specific brand or site.

Record the category next to each term. The same literal phrase can change category across markets, so classify within each market's list separately. If a term is mostly used by people looking for a price in one market and mostly used for education in another, that is a signal to build different pages, not one translated page.

A decision question to run for each term: open the top results in that market's search engine and note what type of page appears (guide, product page, directory, forum, video). If the results are mostly one page type, the format searchers currently see is a reasonable starting point for your page. If the results are mixed, the query may not have a single expected format, so test more than one approach. Record what you see for each query; do not assume the pattern holds for other queries.

## Step 4: Group terms by page, not by keyword count

Localized lists become unmanageable when every variant gets its own article. Instead, group terms that share the same intent and the same answer into one page. For example, two spellings of the same product name and one local colloquial term may all belong to a single product guide, with the primary variant in the title and the others used naturally in headings and body text.

Keep separate pages when:

- the intent differs (a buying guide and a how-to guide are different pages even if they share nouns);
- the regulations, prices, or service details differ between markets;
- the local customer uses a different product name or category that would confuse a single page.

Keep one page when the answer is the same and only the wording changes. Merging pages that answer the same question in the same way reduces duplication and gives each page more context.

## Step 5: Check the facts before you write

A guide written for one market can quote a price, a deadline, a legal rule, or a process that does not apply elsewhere, so localized pages need a market-specific fact check. Before a localized page is published, a reviewer who knows the market should confirm:

- every number, date, price, and threshold;
- every regulation or standard named;
- every product or service detail that varies by country;
- the spelling, units, and currency used.

Keep a short source note for each factual claim, so the reviewer and future editors can trace where the claim came from. A practical rule: if a claim cannot be traced to your own confirmed business facts or to a named authoritative source in that market, remove it or mark it for review.

## Step 6: Measure the result in each market

Localized research is a hypothesis until you measure it. Track these in your own reporting for each market:

- impressions and clicks for the target queries in that country;
- the average position for those queries over time;
- whether the page is receiving the intent you planned for (for example, whether a guide attracts informational clicks while a product page attracts transactional ones).

Compare these metrics only within the same market and period, because search demand and competition can differ between markets. If a localized page gets impressions but few clicks, a conditional check is to compare the title and meta description with the wording your collected customer language uses. If they differ, revise them and compare again within the same market.

## Trade-offs to weigh

Localization improves relevance but adds maintenance. The main trade-offs are:

- **Coverage versus depth.** Fewer markets with thoroughly checked pages can be easier to keep accurate than many thin translations. Thin pages without local facts are hard to keep current.
- **Consistency versus local fit.** A single global brand voice is easier to manage, but local terms and examples may suit their own market better. Decide which elements must stay consistent (brand name, product names, core claims) and which can adapt.
- **Reviewer availability.** Each language needs someone who can check accuracy. If you cannot staff that review, delay the market rather than publish unchecked pages.

## A starting checklist you can run this week

This is a starting suggestion for a first pass, not a recommended standard. Adjust the scope to your capacity.

1. Pick the markets where you already have real demand and can deliver your service. A first pass can be as small as two.
2. For each market, collect a working batch of candidate terms from autocomplete, customer language, and Search Console country filters.
3. Classify each term's intent and note the dominant page type in the search results.
4. Group terms into pages by shared intent and answer.
5. Assign a local reviewer to check facts, spelling, and units for each page.
6. Publish one page per group, then review country-filtered Search Console data after a review period you set before publishing.

The output of this process is not a longer keyword list. It is a short set of pages, each matched to a market, an intent, and a set of facts someone has confirmed.
