## The short answer

You don't have to be the one who writes every blog post for your business to show up in Google. The writing is only one step in a longer chain — figuring out what your customers actually search for, drafting an accurate article about it, checking that the article is actually true, publishing it in the right place, and then watching whether it moves the needle. If you can delegate or automate each of those steps individually, you can keep the parts that need your judgment (confirming facts about your own business, approving what goes out) and hand off the parts that don't (research legwork, first drafts, formatting, publishing, monitoring).

This article walks through what that chain actually looks like, what each link requires, and the decisions you need to make at each step — whether you do it with a person, a freelancer, software, or some mix of the three.

## Why "just write more" isn't the real problem

When a business owner says "I need more customers from Google," what they usually mean is one of three different problems:

1. Google doesn't know their site answers the questions customers are typing in.
2. Google knows, but the pages that could answer those questions aren't good enough to rank.
3. The pages exist and rank, but nobody's kept them updated, so they've drifted down over time.

Writing more articles only solves the first problem, and only if the articles are about things people are actually searching for. If you skip straight to "write more content" without first identifying what your customers search for, you risk ending up with articles that satisfy your own assumptions about your business instead of your customers' actual questions. That's why the sequence below starts before the writing, not at it.

## The five-step chain you need to delegate

### 1. Know what your customers are actually searching for

Before any article gets written, someone (or something) needs to identify real search terms — not guesses about what sounds professional, but the phrasing an actual customer would type into Google. For a hypothetical local dental practice, that might mean "how long does teeth whitening last" or "what to do about a chipped tooth" rather than "cosmetic dentistry services." This step requires looking at your own site's existing pages, your services, and the gap between what you offer and what you've actually written about.

This is a research task, not a writing task, and it's one of the easier pieces to hand off, because it doesn't require anyone to know your business deeply — it requires reading your website and matching it against search behavior.

### 2. Turn a search topic into an accurate first draft

This is the step people usually mean when they say "writing every article." But drafting text is the mechanical middle of the process, not the valuable part. The valuable part is making sure the draft is built from facts that are actually true about your business — your hours, your pricing, what's included in a service, what you do and don't offer.

If you delegate this step to a freelancer or an AI tool without first locking down your business facts, you risk articles that read well but say things that aren't true about your business — a price that's wrong, a service you don't actually offer, a claim you can't back up. If your goal is to build trust with searchers, weigh that risk against the risk of simply publishing less.

### 3. Check the draft against your actual facts before it goes live

Whether a person or software writes the first draft, someone needs to check it against source facts before it's published — not skim it, actually verify each claim traces back to something true about the business. This is the step that's easiest to skip when you're trying to move fast, and skipping it means a wrong claim can go live under your business's name — a credibility problem, not just an SEO problem.

If you're delegating writing to any third party — a person, an agency, or software — ask directly: what confirms the facts in this draft before it's published, and is that confirmation separate from whoever wrote it? A second, independent check is worth asking about before you trust a fast first draft.

### 4. Get it published to the actual site people visit — and confirm it

An approved draft sitting in a document isn't a published article. Someone has to move it into WordPress, or into a GitHub-based site if you're running something like Next.js, format it correctly, and — this is the part that's easy to skip — actually check that the live URL works and shows the article you intended, not a broken page or a draft state.

If you're managing this yourself, decide up front whether you want to review each article before it goes live or let a trusted process publish on a schedule without your daily involvement. Both are reasonable; the difference is how much of your week goes into this step versus the others.

### 5. Watch what happens, and go back to fix what's close but not working

Publishing isn't the finish line. Some pages will land close to page one without quite reaching it — worth improving, not worth ignoring. Your own Google Search Console data is the source of truth here: it can show you which pages are getting impressions but not clicks, or which ones are ranking just outside the range you'd want for a term you care about. If your data shows pages sitting in that near-miss zone, that's a signal to go back and strengthen them rather than move on to a new topic. Checking this regularly is a maintenance task most small teams simply don't get to — not because it's hard, but because it's easy to deprioritize once an article is published and feels "done."

## A decision framework: what should you delegate, and to whom?

Use this as a starting point, not a rule. The right split depends on your time, your budget, and how much you trust the person or tool doing the work.

| Step | Requires business-specific judgment? | Good candidate for delegation? |
|---|---|---|
| Finding what customers search for | Low — mostly pattern-matching against your existing pages | Yes, to a person or software |
| Drafting the article | Low, if facts are supplied first | Yes, once facts are confirmed |
| Confirming facts are correct | High — only you know if it's true | No — keep this with you or a designated approver |
| Publishing to your site | Low — mechanical | Yes |
| Checking rankings and fixing near-misses | Medium — needs review of your own data | Partially — automate the monitoring, review the decisions |

If your own answers to the checklist below skew toward "no one is doing this yet," the table gives you a place to start: automate or delegate the mechanical rows first, and keep the judgment row with a person who knows your business.

## A practical starting checklist

If you're deciding how to structure this for your own business, work through these questions in order:

- **Do you know, in writing, the core facts about your business** — pricing, services, hours, what's included, what's not — that any article would need to be accurate? If not, start here before delegating anything.
- **Who is finding topics your customers actually search for**, and are they checking that against your existing pages so you're not duplicating what you've already covered?
- **Is whoever drafts articles working from your confirmed facts**, or improvising based on general knowledge of your industry?
- **Is there a separate check before publishing** that specifically looks for claims the draft can't support — separate from whoever wrote the draft?
- **After an article goes live, does anyone confirm the actual URL works** and shows the right content?
- **Is anyone reviewing Google Search Console on a schedule** to catch pages that are close to ranking well but need a second pass?

If the answer to most of these is "no one, right now," that's the actual gap — not the writing itself.

## FAQ

**Do I have to give up all control if I delegate the writing?**
No. The chain above separates "who writes the first draft" from "who confirms it's true and approves publishing." You can delegate drafting entirely while keeping fact confirmation and publishing approval with yourself, whether the drafting is done by a person or software.

**What if I only have time to delegate one step?**
Consider starting with topic research and drafting — the two steps that require the least business-specific judgment, as long as you've already written down your core business facts for whoever (or whatever) drafts the article to work from.

**Is it risky to publish on a fixed schedule without reviewing every article?**
That depends on the fact-checking step behind it. A schedule with a real, independent fact check before publishing is different from a schedule with no check at all — ask specifically how that check works before trusting an autopilot setup with your site.
