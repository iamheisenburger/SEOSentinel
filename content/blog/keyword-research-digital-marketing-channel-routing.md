Keyword research in digital marketing is the work of collecting the exact phrases your buyers use and deciding which channel each phrase should serve. The output is a shared vocabulary your whole marketing effort draws on, not a spreadsheet that only the SEO person opens.

That distinction matters most for founders, small business owners and small teams. You probably run search, email, social posts and your own website with the same few people. If each channel invents its own wording, you pay for the same research several times and your message drifts. If you collect phrases once and route them deliberately, one piece of research can inform your blog, your ads, your emails and your product pages.

This guide covers how to structure that routing, how each channel can use a phrase differently, and a check you can run on your own data to see whether your wording matches how customers search. The framework is the author's recommendation, not an industry standard. Test it against your own analytics.

## What keyword research does beyond SEO

A keyword is a record of how a person described a need at the moment they typed it. That record is useful anywhere you need to describe your offer to a stranger. In digital marketing it can do four jobs:

- **Choose topics.** Which questions does your site answer, and which does it ignore?
- **Choose language.** Does your homepage call the product what customers call it, or what your team calls it internally?
- **Choose where to spend effort.** Some phrases suit a long-form article, some a landing page, some a short ad, some none of these.
- **Check results.** Once something is live, the same phrases show whether people find it.

Treating keyword research as only an SEO task limits it to the first job. Treating it as shared vocabulary lets every channel use it.

## Build one master sheet before you touch any channel

Start with a single table, even if it lives in a spreadsheet. Suggested columns:

1. **Phrase**, written exactly as a customer would type or say it.
2. **Where you found it**, such as Search Console, a sales call, a support ticket, a review or an autocomplete suggestion.
3. **What the person wants**, in a few words: learn, compare, buy, fix a problem, find a local provider.
4. **Channel**, the place this phrase will do the most work (see the next section).
5. **Asset**, the specific page, ad group, email or post that will carry it.
6. **Status and last checked**, so stale rows are visible.

The "Where you found it" column is easy to skip, and it's useful. Phrases from your own customers' mouths, such as a question repeated in support tickets, show that real buyers ask it. Phrases from tools or autocomplete show that people type it. Both are worth having, but they're different kinds of evidence, and you should know which one you're relying on.

The "Asset" column forces a decision. A phrase without a planned asset is a wish, not a plan. If you can't name the page, ad or email that will carry it, leave the status as "idea" and move on.

## How each channel can use a phrase differently

The same phrase can play a different role depending on where you use it. These are practical suggestions to adapt, not guarantees of results.

### Organic search content

Here the phrase usually becomes a page topic, a title and the headings that structure the answer. As a rule of thumb, consider giving a longer, more specific question its own article, and let a broad commercial phrase point to a core service or product page. Check this against your own Search Console data: if the queries that already earn impressions for a page match the topic you intended, the page is doing its job; if they don't, the page may need clearer wording or a different owner for that phrase.

A useful discipline is one main topic per page. If two pages on your site chase the same phrase, decide which one owns it and adjust the other so they stop competing.

### Paid search

Paid search uses phrases as targeting, then as ad copy. The phrase decides who sees the ad, and the wording in the ad should echo what the searcher typed. A practical use of your master sheet: when a phrase has a clear buying purpose, test it in an ad group, and watch which exact search terms trigger the ad. Those triggered terms are new rows for the sheet. Paid search can act as a fast feedback loop on language that organic content can then adopt.

Record paid and organic results in separate columns of the sheet, so each channel's results stay readable on their own.

### Email and lifecycle messages

Email doesn't compete for search positions, but the phrases can still help. Consider writing subject lines and opening sentences in the customer's own wording for their problem. If prospects keep asking "how do I move my old blog to a new site", an email titled "Migrating your blog without losing your content" uses vocabulary they already recognise. Pull rows from the sheet that were tagged with a "fix a problem" or "learn" intent and use them as email topics, then check open and click data inside your own email platform to see whether the wording lands.

### Social and community posts

Social platforms and forums are places where people often phrase questions informally. They can be a good source of phrases (see the sourcing section below) and a place to test them. A short post built around a customer's question can show whether the topic interests anyone before you invest in a full article. Treat engagement as a rough signal of interest, not as proof that people will search for it.

### Your own website copy

Compare the headline and first paragraph of your main service or product page with the phrases in your sheet. If customers say "bookkeeping for freelancers" and your page says "financial workflow solutions", you have a vocabulary gap that blog content alone won't close. Fixing it requires editing, not producing, so it's worth checking before you commission new articles.

### Answers from AI assistants

Many people put questions to AI assistants, and these tools can take conversational phrasing. You don't need special research for this. Take the questions in your sheet and ask them yourself, in your own words, across a few assistants. Note whether your business or your content appears and whether what's said about you is accurate. In the author's experience this works best as a spot check rather than a measurement, so repeat a question on a different day and compare what you get. If an assistant describes your product wrongly, review whether your key pages state the relevant facts clearly and explicitly.

## Where the phrases come from

You don't need paid software to fill the sheet. Useful internal sources include:

- **Search Console queries** for your site, showing terms that already produce impressions.
- **Your site's internal search**, if you have one, which shows what visitors couldn't find.
- **Sales and support conversations**: the first question a prospect asks and the question a new customer asks after buying.
- **Reviews, including reviews of competitors**, which show how people describe the outcome they wanted.
- **Autocomplete and related searches** in search engines, for variations on a phrase you already have.

Be cautious about volume figures from any tool. Treat them as estimates, compare them across tools before relying on them, and remember they say nothing about whether the searcher would become your customer. Use them to compare phrases against each other roughly, not as targets.

## A reader-run check: does your wording match how customers search?

Here's a diagnostic you can run with data you already own. It's a recommendation, so adjust it to your situation.

1. **Export your Search Console queries** for the pages you care about most.
2. **Pick a small sample** of the phrases your sales or support conversations produced.
3. **Search for each phrase in your export.** Mark it as "appears", "close variant appears" or "absent".
4. **For absent phrases, check your site.** Is there a page that clearly answers it? If yes, the page may need clearer wording or better internal links. If no, you've found a content gap.
5. **For phrases that appear but sit on an unexpected page**, decide which page should own them and edit accordingly.

To make this concrete, here is a **hypothetical** example. Imagine a small accounting firm whose support emails keep asking "do I need to register for VAT as a sole trader". The firm exports its queries and finds that nothing resembling that phrase appears, and no page addresses it. That's a clear row for the sheet: intent is "learn", channel is organic content, and the asset is a new article. The same row could also feed an email to existing clients and a short social post. This example is invented to show the method; it isn't a result anyone achieved.

The output of the check is a short, ranked list of gaps and mismatches, each tied to an asset. If the list is empty, your wording and your customers' wording are already close, and your time is better spent elsewhere.

## Common mistakes in cross-channel keyword work

- **Researching once and filing it away.** Customer language shifts and your own offer changes. Revisit the sheet on a regular rhythm that matches how quickly your business changes.
- **Using a phrase because it's popular, not because it's yours.** A phrase only helps if your business can honestly serve the person who typed it. Strike rows you can't fulfil.
- **Letting channels diverge.** If ads promise one thing and the landing page says another, you waste the click. Keep the sheet as the single reference for wording.
- **Skipping the feedback loop.** Each channel generates new phrases: paid search terms, email replies, social comments. Feed them back into the sheet.
- **Measuring phrases rather than outcomes.** A phrase that brings visitors who never enquire is a weak row regardless of its popularity. Check what visitors from a phrase do next in your own analytics.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need different keyword research for each channel?

Not usually. You need one source of customer phrases and a clear rule for which channel carries each. Channel-specific testing then adds new phrases to the same sheet.

### Who should own the sheet in a small team?

One named person, even if several people contribute. Shared documents without an owner tend to go stale because nobody is responsible for pruning rows or recording what happened after publishing.

### How do I know a phrase is worth pursuing?

Check three things: whether your business can genuinely serve the searcher, whether you can name the asset that will carry it, and whether your own data later shows visitors from that phrase taking the action you want. If any answer is no, lower its priority.
