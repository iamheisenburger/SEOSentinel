Promote a new blog post in the places where your likely customers already read, ask questions and make recommendations. Those places are:

- your own site
- your email list and customer conversations
- communities where people discuss your topic
- partners and people you mention in the post

Google primarily finds pages through links from other pages it has already crawled, and those links often come from other websites. Google's guidance says links from other sites happen naturally over time and that you can also encourage people to discover your content by promoting your site ([source 1](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide)). Promotion is therefore worth treating as part of publishing, not an optional extra.

This guide orders channels by how directly they connect a post to the right readers. The order is the author's proposed framework, not an industry standard. It also covers how to promote without spamming, how to check the post first, and how to tell which channel worked.

## Why promotion matters after you hit publish

A published post is only a URL. Readers are unlikely to find it unless something points to it. Two kinds of pointers matter:

1. **Links from pages Google already knows.** These include your own pages and other sites. Google's essentials guidance asks site owners to make links crawlable so Google can find other pages on their site through the links on a page ([source 2](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials)).
2. **People who know the post exists.** The same guidance tells site owners to be active in communities where they can tell like-minded people about the services and products they mention on their site ([source 2](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials)).

The channels below cover both. The order reflects effort and relevance.

## Start with your own site

The cheapest promotion is free, immediate and fully under your control.

- **Link from related posts.** Open older posts on adjacent topics and add a natural sentence that links to the new one. Use descriptive anchor text, such as the topic of the new post, rather than "click here".
- **Link from pages that already get visits.** If a service page, pricing page or popular guide covers a related question, a contextual link sends existing visitors to the new post.
- **Use real links.** Google's essentials guidance asks for crawlable links so it can find other pages on your site ([source 2](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials)). Check that your "related posts" block or navigation uses ordinary links, not only buttons that depend on scripts.
- **Don't treat a sitemap as the whole job.** A sitemap is a file listing the URLs on your site that you care about, and some content management systems create one automatically. Google's guidance says a sitemap isn't required ([source 1](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide)).

## Use the audiences you already have

People who already know your business are the easiest to reach, and they may be more willing to share a post that helps them.

### Email list

If you have a newsletter or customer list, send the post to the segment it was written for. Write a short note on the problem the post solves, not just its title. A post about choosing a service plan suits prospects. A post about getting more from a product you already sold suits existing customers.

### Sales and support conversations

Many posts exist because customers keep asking the same question. When it comes up again, reply with a short answer and then link to the post for the full version. This puts the post in front of someone who is looking for that answer right now. It also shows whether the post actually answers the question.

### Your own social profiles

Post a summary of the post's main point rather than a bare link. Pick the network where your buyers are active, and check your analytics to see which networks send visitors.

## Join communities where the question is already being asked

Communities are easy to misuse. Google's guidance encourages being active in communities where you can tell like-minded people about what you offer ([source 2](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials)). The key word is active. Dropping a link and leaving isn't being active.

Relevant places include:

- industry forums and discussion boards
- question-and-answer sites
- professional groups
- community chat spaces for your trade
- local business groups, if you serve a local area

A workable approach, offered as a recommendation:

1. **Find threads where the question matches your post.** Search the community for the problem your post solves.
2. **Answer the question in full in the thread.** The reply should help even if nobody clicks.
3. **Link only when the post adds something.** Add the link as extra depth, such as a calculator, checklist or longer walkthrough.
4. **Follow the community rules.** Some groups ban links. Read the rules before posting.
5. **Come back.** Reply to follow-up questions.

Avoid posting the same message in many communities at once. It looks like spam.

## Reach partners and people you mention

Links from other sites can't be forced, but you can give people a reason to link.

- **Tell people you cited.** If the post quotes an expert, a customer or a supplier, let them know. They may share it, and a mention costs them nothing.
- **Notify the owners of resources you link to.** This works best when your post adds something to their topic, such as a newer angle or a clearer explanation.
- **Offer a contribution.** Where a newsletter, association or industry site publishes guest material or roundups, offer a piece that fits their audience instead of pitching your post.
- **Tell complementary businesses.** If your post helps their customers, a short note with the link gives them something to pass on.

Keep these messages short and specific, and say why the post is relevant to that person's audience. Prioritise relevant sites over a long list of irrelevant ones.

## Repurpose the post instead of repeating the link

Posting the same link again and again gives your audience nothing new. Repurposing gives each channel something different:

- **A short summary** of the main answer for social profiles
- **A single checklist or table** from the post for a community answer
- **A section pulled into an email**, with a link for the rest
- **A worked example** from the post for a sales or support reply

Each piece should stand alone, with the post as the "read more" for people who want the detail.

## Check the post before you promote it

Promotion shows whatever the post contains to more people, including mistakes. Google's guidance on helpful content asks whether the content was written or reviewed by an expert or enthusiast who demonstrably knows the topic, and whether it has any easily verified factual errors ([source 3](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)). Those are good questions to answer before you send the post to people who know your field.

A quick pre-promotion check, which you run yourself:

- Are names, prices, dates, features and claims about your own business correct today?
- Does every statistic appear in the source you cite?
- Would a reader in a community thread find the post useful, or does it read like an advert?
- Does the post answer the question near the top, so a visitor from a forum thread sees value quickly?

If the post fails any check, fix it before you promote it.

## Choose channels with a simple decision test

You can't use every channel for every post. This table is a recommended way to choose, based on who the post is for:

| If the post is... | Start with | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Aimed at existing customers | Email list, support replies | The audience already knows you |
| Aimed at people comparing options | Sales conversations, relevant communities | Readers are actively deciding |
| A deep how-to guide | Partner outreach, community answers | Other sites may link to practical resources |
| Built on original information or a customer story | Partners and people mentioned, social profiles | The people involved may be willing to share it |
| A general explainer on a broad topic | Internal links, community answers | Focus on being found rather than pushed |

Pick the channels the table points to for each post and do them properly. Record the choice, so you can compare results later.

## Measure what each channel did

Promotion you don't measure is guesswork. Keep a small log with one row per post:

- the date published and the channels you used
- where you shared it and when
- visits that arrived from each place, using your analytics tool
- queries and pages in Google Search Console as time passes
- any links or mentions you gained, and from whom

Here is a hypothetical example, not real data. You publish a guide, answer two community threads and email your list. Later, your analytics show visits from the email and one thread, and none from the other. That tells you which channel to spend your next hour on. Visits from a channel are not proof of any effect on rankings, so treat them as a guide to where to put effort.

Review the log on a regular schedule you set. If a channel keeps sending no visits, test a different approach or drop it.
