AI-written content can be safe for SEO when it is accurate, useful and original, and when you are not publishing it in bulk to manipulate rankings. Google says using AI gives content no special gains: "It's just content" [2]. The practical risk is not the tool. It is publishing unchecked claims, and Google says it is critical to manually fact-check and review all AI-generated content before publishing [1].

## What Google says about AI-generated content

As of November 2026, Google's guidance treats AI content like any other content. If it is useful, helpful, original and satisfies aspects of E-E-A-T, it might do well in Search. If it doesn't, it might not [2]. Google adds that generative AI can be particularly useful for researching a topic and adding structure to original content [1].

The limit is scale without value. Using generative AI tools to generate many pages without adding value for users may violate Google's spam policy on scaled content abuse [1]. That policy covers many pages generated for the primary purpose of manipulating Search rankings rather than helping users, and it applies no matter how the content is created [3]. Google's long-standing position is that automation, including generative AI, is spam if the primary purpose is manipulating ranking [3]. Pages that violate spam policies may rank lower or not appear in results at all [3].

Two further points from Google's guidance:

- **Disclosure:** Google says AI or automation disclosures are useful where a reader might think "How was this created?", and to consider adding one when it would be reasonably expected [2]. It does not present a mandatory label.
- **Bylines:** Giving AI an author byline is probably not the best way to make clear that AI was part of the process [2].

## Is AI content safe for SEO? The real risks

Each risk below traces back to Google's own wording.

- **Factual errors.** Generative models don't retrieve facts. They predict a likely sequence of words based on training data, so outputs may contain inaccuracies, also called hallucinations [1].
- **Thin or generic pages.** Google's quality rater guidelines describe main content created with little to no effort, originality or added value (section 4.6.6) [1]. Raters' ratings don't directly influence ranking, but they help evaluate Google's ranking systems [1].
- **Scaled content abuse.** Large amounts of unoriginal content that gives users little to no value are the target, however they are produced [3]. Google's systems, including SpamBrain, analyze patterns and signals to identify spam however it is produced [2].
- **E-E-A-T gaps.** Content that is not helpful, original and trustworthy might not do well [2]. On topics like health, civic or financial information, Google's systems place even greater emphasis on signals of reliability [2].

A wrong fact hurts twice: it misleads the reader and it undermines the trust signals Google says it looks for.

## Why facts need checking before publishing

- **Invented statistics:** a precise percentage with no origin.
- **Fake citations:** a study, report or URL that looks real but does not exist or does not say what the draft claims.
- **Wrong dates:** a law, release or event placed in the wrong year, or an old fact presented as current.

- **Confident wrong names:** the wrong person, company, tool or job title attached to a real fact.

Google's review requirement is wider than the body text. It also applies to metadata that can appear in Search results: title elements, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text [1].

## Step-by-step fact-check process

Work through the draft in this order:

1. **List every claim.** Read the draft and extract each sentence containing a number, date, name, rule, price, quote, or cause-and-effect statement. Include the title, meta description, and alt text [1].
2. **Tag each claim by type.** Use labels such as statistic, date, product detail, quote, legal or policy rule, or name. The tag tells you which kind of source can settle it.
3. **Verify each claim against a primary source.** Go to the body that owns the fact: the government agency, the company's own page, or the standards body. Fact-checkers read "laterally" across many websites rather than digging deep into one [4], so open a second site to learn who is behind the first.
4. **Trace to the original.** Follow claims, quotes and media back to the original context [4]. A statistic quoted in five articles may all come from one misread source.
5. **Record the source.** Write the URL, the page title, and the date you checked it next to each claim.
6. **Fix or remove what you cannot verify.** If no primary source supports a claim, delete it or rewrite it as a statement you can support. Do not keep a claim because it sounds right.
7. **Re-check after edits.** Every rewrite can introduce a new unverified fact. Run the claim list again on the final text, then on the published page.

### Example

This is a hypothetical draft for a dental clinic, used only to show the log. The draft says: "Our clinic opened in 2012. Whitening lasts about a year. Dr. Lee leads the team."

| Claim | Type | Check | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opened in 2012 | Date | Owner's confirmed business facts | Confirmed, logged |
| Whitening lasts about a year | Statistic | No primary source found | Removed |
| Dr. Lee leads the team | Name | Clinic's team page | Wrong name, corrected |

Three claims were listed, one was confirmed, one removed and one corrected, so all three are resolved before publishing.

## Tools and methods for verifying claims

Match the method to the claim type:

- **Primary sources.** Government pages, company pages and standards bodies beat any article that summarizes them. Start here for prices, rules and specifications.
- **Archived pages.** When a cited page has changed or vanished, an archived copy shows what it said and when, which helps catch outdated details.
- **Quote and reverse image search.** Search a distinctive phrase in quotation marks to find its first appearance, and run a reverse image search on photos. This is the "trace to the original" step in SIFT: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims, quotes and media to the original context [4].
- **A second human reviewer.** Someone who did not write or prompt the draft checks the claim log against the sources. Fresh eyes catch the claim the first reviewer already believed.
- **An independent AI pass.** A separate AI check that has to find support for each claim can flag gaps quickly. Treat it as a screen that narrows what the human reviews, not as the final authority, since Google asks for manual review [1].

## Signals that show human expertise and trust

Google's guidance points to several visible signals:

- **Author bylines.** Use accurate bylines where readers would reasonably expect them, for any content where someone might think "Who wrote this?" [2]. Name a real person who is accountable for the page.
- **First-hand experience.** Add what only your business knows: your process, your customers' questions, your own numbers. Generic text cannot supply this.
- **Cited sources.** Link to the primary sources you verified so readers can inspect the evidence.
- **Dated updates.** Show when the page was last reviewed and what changed.
- **Editorial review notes.** A short line on how the piece was produced and reviewed gives context. Google suggests explaining how automation was used where that makes sense for your audience [1].

## Publishing and monitoring after the fact check

Publishing is not the end of verification. Search Console is where Google contacts site owners about a spam manual action, with a notice to the registered account, and where you can apply for reconsideration [3]. Use it as your monitoring base:

1. After publishing, confirm the live page matches the checked version, including title, description and alt text.
2. Review each new page's performance in Search Console's performance data at a regular interval you can sustain, such as weekly or monthly.
3. When a page loses impressions, clicks or position, re-read it for outdated facts first, then for gaps against what now ranks.
4. If you find an error later, correct the page, update the visible review date and log the new source.

## Verifying claims that come from the business's own website and product data

Many AI drafts fail on the facts closest to home: your prices, services, locations, team and plan limits. A model that reads your site can repeat a stale page as if it were current. Treat your own data like any other source:

1. **Confirm each fact with the owner before publishing.** One accountable person signs off on the business profile the articles draw from.
2. **Keep a source log.** Link each claim to the specific page of your site it came from.
3. **Re-verify when details change.** A new price, a closed location or a discontinued service should trigger a re-check of every article that mentions it.
