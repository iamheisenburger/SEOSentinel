An AI blog post generator is software that turns a topic, keyword or prompt into a written draft. The draft is the easy part. Whether that draft is accurate, useful to your customers and safe to publish depends on what you feed the generator and what you check afterwards.

Google's guidance on generative AI content says the technology can be particularly useful when researching a topic and adding structure to original content. It also warns that using generative tools to generate many pages without adding value for users may violate its spam policy on scaled content abuse [1]. Both statements matter when you pick a tool and decide how to run it.

This guide explains what a generator does, why its drafts contain mistakes, what to give it before it writes, and how to review the output. It ends with a short test you can run on any tool using your own business.

## What an AI blog post generator actually does

Generators differ more in what surrounds the writing than in the writing itself. As a working recommendation (the author's own grouping, not an industry standard), it helps to sort them into three types:

1. **Prompt-box writers.** You type a topic and receive a draft. Everything else is yours: choosing the topic, supplying facts, checking claims, formatting, publishing and measuring.
2. **Template or outline writers.** You pick a format such as a how-to or a listicle, and the tool fills it. You still own facts, review and publishing.
3. **Pipeline tools.** The tool handles several stages, such as finding topics, drafting, checking and publishing. You own the approvals and the business facts it works from.

The type matters because the hard work in blogging is not typing sentences. It is deciding what customers search for, saying only true things about your business, and getting the page live in the right place. A prompt-box writer saves time on one step. A pipeline tool covers more steps, which means you need to inspect how each step is controlled.

## Why AI drafts contain mistakes

Generative models don't retrieve facts. They predict a likely sequence of words based on their training data, so their output may contain inaccuracies, often called hallucinations [1]. This is a property of how the models work, not a flaw in one brand.

When you review a draft, look first at the places where an error costs you most. This is the author's checklist, not a published taxonomy:

- **Statistics and study claims.** Confirm each number appears in the source it cites. If there is no source, cut the number.
- **Prices, plans and guarantees.** These are details about your own business that the model can't know unless you told it. Compare each with your confirmed facts.
- **Product or service capabilities.** Remove any feature you don't offer or integration that doesn't exist.
- **Dates and "current" claims.** Check anything described as current against today's facts.
- **Metadata.** Titles, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text can carry errors too, and they can appear in search results [1].

Google's guidance says it is critical to manually fact-check and review all AI-generated content for accuracy and trustworthiness before publishing. It adds that this review also applies to metadata such as title elements, meta description elements, structured data and alternate texts for images [1]. Treat that as the minimum for any generator, whether it is a free tool or a paid platform.

## What to give a generator before it writes

A generator can only be as specific as its input. A one-line prompt tends to produce generic text because the tool has nothing about your business to draw on. This is a recommended brief, not a required format.

**Who the reader is and what they are trying to do.** "A homeowner comparing two repair options" calls for a different post from "a facilities manager writing a budget".

**The search query the post should answer.** Pick one specific question a customer would type, and build the post around it.

**Confirmed facts about your business.** List what you actually offer, your real prices or pricing approach, service areas, policies and anything you refuse to claim. If the generator can't see these, it has to fill the gap with guesses.

**Sources for every outside claim.** If a statistic belongs in the post, supply the source yourself and check the number appears there. If you can't find a source, leave the statistic out.

**The next step for the reader.** Decide whether the post should lead to a booking page, a sign-up page or a contact form, so the ending does something useful.

If you cannot list your confirmed facts, pause before generating anything. A post written without them is a post you will have to rewrite while fact-checking.

## A review routine for every draft

Review is where a draft becomes publishable. Use the same routine every time so it doesn't depend on your mood or deadline. Work through these checks in order.

**Highlight every checkable claim.** Mark numbers, percentages, named studies, prices, legal or medical statements, and any sentence beginning "research shows". Each one needs a source you can open or a fact you can confirm yourself.

**Check claims against the source, not against memory.** Open the cited page and find the exact figure. A statistic that is plausible, or that appears on a different page, is not verified.

**Check statements about your own business.** Compare prices, plan details, service lists and guarantees with what you know to be true today. Delete anything you can't confirm.

**Review the metadata.** Read the title, meta description, any structured data and image alt text as carefully as the body, since these elements can appear in search results [1].

**Ask the value question.** Would a customer learn something useful from this page that they couldn't get from the top few results? If the honest answer is no, add a real example, a decision rule or a detail from your own work, or don't publish.

**Check it after it goes live.** Open the published URL and confirm the right article appears, with the right title, formatting and links.

## Scale is where generators get risky

The temptation with a generator is volume: if one post is quick to produce, why not publish far more? Google's guidance warns that using generative tools to generate many pages without adding value for users may violate its spam policy on scaled content abuse [1].

The practical point is that volume alone is not the issue. Pages that add nothing for users are. Here is a hypothetical example, invented for illustration. A small accounting firm publishes a long run of posts titled "Tax tips for [city]" that differ only in the city name, with no local detail. Each page is thin and repetitive, and none gives a reader a reason to prefer it. By contrast, a smaller set of posts that each answer a distinct client question, written from the firm's real services and fees, gives every page a purpose.

A sensible rule is to set your publishing pace by how much you can verify and how many distinct customer questions you can answer, not by how fast the tool writes. If your review routine is the bottleneck, slow the pace until the routine holds.

## A short test for any AI blog post generator

Before you commit to a tool, run this test with a topic you know thoroughly. It uses your own knowledge as the benchmark, so no outside data is needed.

1. **Give it your real business details** and one specific customer question.
2. **Generate one post** and highlight every checkable claim.
3. **Count the claims you can't verify** from a source or from your own knowledge. The count shows how much checking the tool leaves to you.
4. **Check whether it flags uncertainty or enforces sources**, or whether it states everything with equal confidence.
5. **Look at the workflow around the draft.** Can you review before publishing? Can it publish to your site? Does it confirm the page is live?
6. **Read the metadata** it produced, not only the body.

A tool with a low count at step 3 and a review option at step 5 is easier to trust. A tool that writes fluent text but leaves you to discover the errors puts the full burden of Google's fact-check guidance on you. Decide whether your own review capacity can carry that burden at the pace you plan to publish.

## Questions worth settling before you start

**Should you disclose that a post was AI-assisted?** Google's guidance on generative AI content tells site owners to focus on accuracy, quality and relevance [1]. Check the current guidance and any rules that apply to your industry or region.

**Is a generator enough on its own?** For a first draft, it can be. For a post that leaves your site with your name on it, the review routine above is what makes it publishable.

**What should you automate first?** Start with the stages that are mechanical, such as formatting and publishing, and keep human attention on the facts. Move toward fuller automation only when your review process consistently catches nothing new.
