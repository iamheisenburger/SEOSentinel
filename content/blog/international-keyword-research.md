## What international keyword research actually means

International keyword research is the process of finding the terms people search for in a given country or language before you write content aimed at that market. It is not the same as translating your existing keyword list. A phrase that converts well in one country can be phrased completely differently, searched at a different volume, or carry a different intent in another — even when the underlying language is the same.

If your business sells into more than one country, or you're deciding whether to expand your content into a new language, the question isn't "how do I translate my keywords?" It's "what do people in this market actually type into Google when they have the problem my product solves?" Those are different questions, and treating them as the same one is the most common mistake teams make when they go international with content.

This guide walks through a practical, evidence-based way to approach international keyword research without assuming you have access to expensive enterprise tooling — and without assuming translation equals research.

## Why translating keywords doesn't work

Consider a hypothetical example: a U.S.-based SaaS company that sells "project management software" wants to expand into the UK, Australia, and Germany. If the marketing team simply translates "project management software" into German ("Projektmanagement-Software") and calls that their German keyword strategy, they've skipped several steps that matter:

- **Local phrasing differs.** Even within English-speaking markets, UK searchers might use different terms than US searchers for the same concept (for example, "estate agent" vs. "real estate agent"). A direct port of US keywords to a UK content plan can miss how people there actually search.
- **Search intent shifts by market.** A term might be purely informational in one country and heavily commercial in another, depending on how mature that market is for your category.
- **Competitive and cultural context changes.** The reasons someone searches for a topic, and what they expect to find when they click, can vary by region — shaped by local regulation, pricing norms, or established competitors in that market.
- **Literal translation can produce phrases native speakers never actually use**, even if they're grammatically correct.

This is a hypothetical illustration meant to show the reasoning, not a documented case — but the underlying logic (that language ≠ search behavior) is something you can verify yourself using the checks below.

## A framework for approaching international keyword research

Rather than assuming any single tool or workflow, treat international keyword research as a repeatable, market-by-market process. Here is a recommended sequence — treat it as a starting framework to adapt, not a fixed industry standard.

### Step 1: Define the market, not just the language

Before you research keywords, be explicit about which *market* you're targeting — country, not just language. "Spanish" is not a market; "Spanish speakers in Mexico" and "Spanish speakers in Spain" are two different markets with different search engines' regional behavior, different currencies, different regulations, and often different vocabulary for the same product category.

Write down, for each market:
- The country (or countries) you're targeting
- The primary language(s) searchers in that market use
- Whether you already have any customers, traffic, or sales data from that market you can check against

### Step 2: Start from your own site's existing international signals

If your site already gets any traffic from a target country — even a small amount — your Google Search Console data is the single most reliable starting point available to you, because it reflects real search behavior rather than assumption. Filter your Search Console performance report by country and look at:
- Which queries already bring in clicks or impressions from that country
- Which of those queries have high impressions but low click-through rate (a signal the page ranks but doesn't match what the searcher expected)
- Which pages already rank on page two for that country's search results — these are often the cheapest wins in a new market because you've already earned some relevance signal there

This step is a reader-run check: log into Search Console, filter by country, sort by impressions, and see what shows up. You may find the market has more organic signal already than you assumed.

### Step 3: Talk to native speakers before you research volume

Autocomplete, "People also ask" boxes, and related-search suggestions on the local version of Google (accessed by changing the country and language settings, or using a VPN set to that country) will surface real phrasing patterns. But autocomplete alone can mislead you if you don't know the market. Before finalizing a keyword list for a new country, have a native speaker or someone with market familiarity review the list and flag:
- Phrases that are grammatically correct but not how a real customer would say it
- Regional slang or product category names that differ from your home market
- Terms that carry a different connotation in that market (a word that's neutral in one country can carry buying hesitation or negative connotation elsewhere)

This is a qualitative check, not a data pull, but it catches errors that no keyword tool will catch, because tools show you what's searched, not why it sounds natural or unnatural.

### Step 4: Separate informational, navigational, and transactional intent per market

Search intent for the same underlying topic can differ by market maturity. In a country where your product category is new, most searches may be informational ("what is X," "how does X work"). In a country where the category is established and competitors already exist, searches skew transactional ("best X," "X pricing," "X vs Y").

For each market, sort candidate keywords into three buckets:
1. **Informational** — early-stage, educational queries
2. **Comparison/consideration** — "X vs Y," "best X for [use case]"
3. **Transactional** — "buy," "pricing," "near me," "sign up"

If a market skews heavily informational, your first content wave there should be educational, not sales-focused — publishing a pricing comparison page into a market that has never heard of your category is a common way to waste the first few articles you publish there.

### Step 5: Check for false cognates and category-name mismatches

A specific and underrated risk in international keyword research is the false cognate: a word that looks or sounds like an English term but means something different, or refers to a different product category, in another market. For example, terms that describe a product category cleanly in one English-speaking market can refer to something else entirely, or not exist at all, in another region's vernacular. The only reliable way to catch this is the native-speaker review in Step 3 — no automated tool distinguishes intent-carrying nuance from literal translation.

### Step 6: Build a small pilot before committing a full content calendar

Rather than committing to a large content plan in a brand-new market immediately, a conservative approach is to publish a small batch of articles targeting your highest-confidence keywords in that market — ones you've validated through Search Console signal (Step 2) and native-speaker review (Step 3) — and observe what actually gets impressions and clicks over the following weeks. Treat this as a reader-run diagnostic: publish a small pilot batch, track Search Console performance by country and query for that batch specifically, and let that data — not the initial keyword list — determine whether to scale up.

## A checklist for going international with content

Use this as a working checklist when planning content for a new country or language market:

- [ ] Defined the specific country/market (not just "the language")
- [ ] Pulled existing Search Console data filtered by that country, if any exists
- [ ] Generated a candidate keyword list from local autocomplete and related searches (local Google settings, not your home market's)
- [ ] Had a native speaker or market-familiar reviewer sanity-check the list for natural phrasing and false cognates
- [ ] Sorted keywords into informational / comparison / transactional buckets appropriate to that market's category maturity
- [ ] Chosen a small pilot batch of articles rather than a full calendar
- [ ] Set a review date to check pilot performance in Search Console before committing further budget

## How this connects to ongoing publishing decisions

Once you have a validated keyword list for a market, the next practical questions are usually about production and cadence — how many articles to publish, on what schedule, and how to verify facts stay accurate for a market you may not be fluent in yourself. Those are separate operational decisions from the research described here, but they depend directly on getting this research step right first: a well-run publishing schedule built on the wrong keywords for a market will not produce results, no matter how consistent it is.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the same keyword need different content in every market?**
Not always. If two markets share a language and similar search intent for a topic, you may be able to adapt one article for both with minor localization (currency, examples, regulations). But you should verify this per-topic using the intent-bucket check in Step 4 rather than assuming it holds for every keyword.

**Should I use a country-code domain, subdirectory, or subdomain for international content?**
That's a technical/architecture decision separate from keyword research itself, and it depends on factors like hosting setup, existing domain authority, and how many markets you plan to support — it's worth researching independently before you commit to a URL structure.

**How do I know if a market is worth researching at all?**
Start with Step 2: check whether you already have any organic signal from that country in Search Console, and check whether you have any existing customer or sales data from there. If both are at zero, treat the market as untested rather than assuming it will or won't perform, and consider a very small pilot before investing further.
