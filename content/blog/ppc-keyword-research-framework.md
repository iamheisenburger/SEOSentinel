## What PPC Keyword Research Actually Means

PPC keyword research is the process of finding and organizing the search terms you want your paid ads to appear for, then deciding how much each term is worth to you based on what a click is likely to cost and what it's likely to produce. Unlike organic keyword research, where the goal is matching long-term content to search intent, PPC keyword research has a harder edge: every keyword you choose has an immediate, measurable price attached to it, and a poor choice can cost money starting the moment your campaign goes live.

That difference changes how you should approach the work. In organic search, a keyword that doesn't convert well today might still be worth targeting because it builds topical authority or compounds over time. In paid search, a keyword that doesn't convert is a direct leak in your budget. This guide walks through one practical way to build a PPC keyword list, group it, and watch for the mistakes that quietly drain ad spend. Treat it as a framework to test against your own account data, not a guaranteed formula.

## Start With Intent, Not Volume

The instinct for most people starting PPC keyword research is to look for the highest-volume terms related to their product. This is usually the wrong starting point. Search volume tells you how many people type a phrase into Google; it tells you nothing about whether those people are ready to buy, compare, or just browse.

For paid search, intent matters more than volume because you pay for every click regardless of what that visitor was actually looking for. One way to sort candidate keywords is by where the searcher likely sits in their decision — treat the three categories below as a sorting test to run against your own keyword list, not a settled industry classification:

- **Transactional intent** — the searcher appears close to a decision. Phrases like "buy," "pricing," "for [specific use case]," "near me," or a competitor's brand name combined with "alternative" often fall here. These are commonly treated as higher-priority PPC targets because the gap between click and conversion tends to be shorter.
- **Commercial investigation intent** — the searcher appears to be comparing options. Phrases like "best," "vs," "review," or "top [category] tools" fall here. These can work in PPC but usually benefit from a landing page built for comparison rather than a generic homepage.
- **Informational intent** — the searcher wants to learn, not necessarily buy. Phrases like "what is," "how does," or "guide to" are generally less efficient PPC targets if most clicks won't convert in the same session. If your search terms report shows this pattern for a given phrase, it's a candidate for organic content instead of paid ads.

Run every keyword you find through this three-way filter before it reaches a campaign, and check your own conversion data afterward to confirm which category actually performs for your account.

## Build the List: Four Sources Worth Combining

No single source gives you a complete picture. A reasonably thorough PPC keyword list usually draws from at least four places:

**1. Your own search query reports.** If you're already running any paid campaigns, your ad platform's search terms report shows you the literal phrases people typed before clicking your ad — including ones you never explicitly targeted.

**2. Autocomplete and "people also ask" style suggestions.** Typing your seed terms into a search engine and recording what the autocomplete dropdown suggests is a free, direct way to see what real searchers are typing right now. It won't give you volume or cost data, but it can surface long-tail variations and modifiers (location, price, use case) that a keyword tool might miss.

**3. Competitor ad copy and landing pages.** Searching your core terms and noting which competitors show up in the paid results — and what headlines and offers they're running — tells you which keywords competitors consider worth bidding on in your category. If several competitors are consistently bidding on a term, that's worth investigating further, even without exact performance numbers.

**4. Customer language.** Support tickets, sales call notes, and onboarding questions often contain the exact phrases customers use to describe their problem — phrases that may never show up in a keyword tool because they're too specific or too new.

## Group Keywords Into Ad Groups That Match One Intent Each

A common mistake is dumping dozens of loosely related keywords into one ad group with one generic ad and one landing page. This tends to produce weaker relevance between query, ad, and page than a tighter structure would.

The fix is tight thematic grouping: each ad group should contain keywords that share the same intent and could reasonably point to the same ad copy and landing page. As a hypothetical illustration, a company selling project management software might end up with a group structure like this:

| Ad group theme | Example keyword pattern | Landing page focus |
|---|---|---|
| Brand + competitor comparison | "[competitor] alternative" | Direct comparison page |
| Feature-specific | "[product category] with time tracking" | Feature page |
| Use-case specific | "[product category] for remote teams" | Use-case landing page |
| High-intent transactional | "[product category] pricing" | Pricing page |

Tighter groups mean the searcher's query, your ad headline, and your landing page all say roughly the same thing — you can check whether that alignment matters for your account by comparing relevance or quality signals between a loose group and a tight one.

## Negative Keywords: The Other Half of the List

PPC keyword research isn't finished when you've built your target list — you also need a negative keyword list, which tells the ad platform which searches should never trigger your ad. This is one place where budget can leak silently if it's skipped.

Common categories worth considering for exclusion:

- **Job-seeker terms** — "jobs," "careers," "salary" — if you sell a product, not employment.
- **Free/DIY terms** — "free," "template," "how to build your own" — if your product is paid and not a DIY alternative.
- **Irrelevant modifiers** — terms that share a word with your product but mean something different (a "pipeline" software company, for instance, would want to exclude oil and gas "pipeline" searches).
- **Already-covered brand terms** — if you're bidding on broad or phrase match, your own brand name might already be captured elsewhere; duplicate spend here is often wasted.

Review your search terms report on a cadence that fits your campaign stage, and add any irrelevant query that triggered your ad to the negative list.

## Match Types: How You Research Should Match How You Bid

Keyword research for PPC isn't complete without deciding match type, because the same keyword behaves differently depending on how tightly it's matched:

- **Exact match** targets the keyword and very close variants only — narrowest reach, most predictable.
- **Phrase match** targets searches that include your keyword phrase with other words around it — a middle ground.
- **Broad match** targets any search the platform judges as related in intent — widest reach, highest risk of irrelevant clicks.

One approach for building out PPC keyword research from scratch: start new, untested keywords in phrase or exact match so you can see what triggers your ad before loosening the reins. Consider broad match only for terms you've already validated convert for your account, and pair any broad match keyword with a negative keyword list to help control for drift. Test this against your own results before adopting it as a default.

## A Simple Checklist Before You Launch a Campaign

Before turning on a PPC campaign, it's worth confirming the keyword research behind it covers these points:

1. Every keyword has been sorted into transactional, commercial, or informational intent — and informational terms have been deprioritized or removed.
2. Keywords are grouped into tight ad groups, each mapped to one specific landing page.
3. A negative keyword list exists and excludes obviously irrelevant categories (jobs, free, unrelated meanings).
4. Match types are assigned deliberately, not left on a platform default.
5. You have a plan to review the search terms report on a set schedule, not just at campaign launch.

This isn't a one-time task. Search behavior shifts, new competitors enter auctions, and your own search terms report will keep surfacing phrases you didn't originally plan for. Treat PPC keyword research as a recurring review rather than a single setup step, and revisit the list whenever cost-per-click or conversion rate on a given ad group changes in a way you can see in your own account data.

The research you do for PPC and the research you'd do for organic search overlap more than they diverge — both start from understanding what your prospective customers actually type into a search bar. The split typically comes after that point: informational and early-stage queries that don't justify a cost-per-click are often better answered with an organic article than a paid ad, since you're not paying per click for organic rankings once a page is live.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is keyword research for PPC different from SEO keyword research?**
The sources and discovery methods overlap heavily, but the filtering criteria differ. PPC research weighs intent and estimated cost-per-click heavily because every click has a direct price; SEO research weighs ranking difficulty and long-term content fit because the "cost" is time and effort rather than a per-click fee.

**How many keywords should be in one ad group?**
There's no fixed number that works universally, but one practical test is tighter rather than looser: if the keywords in a group would require noticeably different ad copy or different landing pages to feel relevant, they belong in separate groups.

**Should I include my own brand name as a PPC keyword?**
Many advertisers do, mainly to control the message a searcher sees when they look up the brand directly and to limit competitors from occupying that search result. Whether it's worth the spend depends on how much competitor bidding exists on your brand term and how much of that traffic you're already capturing organically — something only your own account data can confirm.
